I

The Premise

Keep & Crown is a medieval castle-and-kingdom game. You begin with a lone keep and a handful of peasants on a patch of open land. From there you fell timber, quarry stone, mine iron, sow fields, raise houses and walls, train soldiers, climb a tree of knowledge, and grow a realm — all in real time, alongside rivals doing the same.

What makes it more than a siege game is how you win. There is no single victory by conquest. Instead there are four crowns — four completely different ambitions — and any of them takes the game:

The Merchant

Economic

Amass a vast treasury and hold it.

The Faithful

Religious

Convert your people to the faith.

The Scholar

Science

Complete the tree and raise wonders.

The Sovereign

Domain

Grow a great population across many lands.

An army is a tool every path can use — to defend, to raid, to seize land — but it is never a fifth way to win. You can crush a rival's castle and still lose the game to their gold or their faith. And you can't crush them out: you cannot kill the king. Raze a realm's last keep and its ruler flees into exile and refounds elsewhere (see Spies & War) — a brutal setback, never an elimination. That is the whole idea: pick an ambition, race toward it, and defend it while three rivals chase their own.

Playing together

The game runs the same on every machine down to the last coin, so you and your friends stay perfectly in sync over a network — one player hosts, the others join. You can also play solo against the computer to learn the ropes. Fog of war hides what your scouts haven't found, so no one can peek at your plans.

II

The Screen at a Glance

Everything you need sits at the edges of the field; the middle is your realm.

The top bar

A running tally of your realm: Wood, Stone, Food, Iron, your Population (used / housing, with idlers noted), Army size, Gold, Approval, Faith, and banked Research. When your larder can't cover the meals you've ordered, it flashes ⚠ STARVING.

The calendar

Top-right, a Year · Month clock marks the passing seasons — a month every twenty seconds — so you can feel the game's tempo at a glance. A match opens on Year 1, Month 1.

The build palette & panels

Along the bottom sits the build palette. Click a building, then click the ground to raise it. Click a building you already own — a keep, barracks, or market — to open its panel. Buttons top-right open the Goals, Tech, and Spies screens (or press G, C, X).

Fog of war

You see only what your units and buildings can see; ground you've explored is remembered but goes dark once you leave. You can't order an attack on a foe you can't see — which is exactly why a Scout is worth its weight. Playing solo, F toggles fog off to explore freely.

Marching orders

A plain move takes the direct path. Two options ride on top of it:

Shift + right-clickQueue a waypoint — a stop the unit walks to after the current one. Chain as many as you like to steer a march; blue flags mark the stops.
Alt + right-clickCautious march — route around enemies rather than through them (amber flags), and keep re-routing if new danger appears. Combine with Shift for a cautious multi-stop route.

The clever bit: a cautious unit keeps re-planning as it goes. Send it off down a road that looks clear and it will still bend away from an ambush that only reveals itself later — but only around foes it can actually see. Scout first, and your armies march home the safe way.

On a Mac, Alt is the ⌥ Option key.

Pausing together

A solo game pauses the instant you press P — the whole field freezes and the clock stops until you press it again. Handy for a phone call, a think, or a cup of tea.

A networked game can't be frozen by one player alone — that would just leave everyone else waiting. So P instead casts a pause vote, and the rule is simple and fair both ways:

To pauseEveryone must agree. The first P is a proposal (“1/2 agreed”); the game keeps running until the last player presses P too. Then it freezes for all of you, on the same instant.
To resumeEveryone must agree again. While frozen, each player presses P when they're ready; the match runs on only once the last person does. A single hold-out keeps it paused.
While frozenNo orders — you can't command a paused game — but the camera still pans, so you can look around while you talk it over. The calendar, cooldowns and the victory clock all hold exactly where they were: a pause costs no one their tempo.

Changed your mind before everyone agreed? Press P again to withdraw your vote. Since the game holds no in-app chat yet, sort out when to pause the old-fashioned way — a quick word over voice or phone.

Saving, loading & restarting

While the game is paused, three buttons appear on the pause panel: Save game, Load last save and Restart match. Save writes the whole match — every castle, unit, stockpile, tech and score — to a file. Load brings back your most recent save; you stay paused, so press P to pick the game back up. Restart begins the same matchup fresh from Year 1 — it asks for a second click to confirm, since it wipes a game that may be hours old, then the rematch simply starts.

In a networked match the host is the one who loads or restarts: the world is sent to everyone at once (the same way a rejoining player is caught up), so both players resume from the identical position — no chance of two machines running different games. Either player can Save their own copy. Because these only ever happen while paused, they can never knock the two of you out of step.

Leaving a match — hand your realm to the AI

Real life calls, and a two-player game shouldn't collapse because one player has to go. In a networked match a Leave button sits at the top-right. Press it and you're asked which computer opponent should inherit your realm — Easy, Normal or Hard. Choose one and the AI takes over exactly what you built: your castle, your army, your stockpiles, your standing on every path. No fresh-start bonus, no reset — it simply plays on in your place, and you can close the window.

For the player who stays, the match doesn't skip a beat: a banner reads “Player 2 left — the AI now holds their realm,” and the game continues as a duel against the computer.

If someone just vanishes — a crashed game, a dropped connection — the match pauses and holds their place. After a few seconds the player left behind sees “Opponent disconnected,” and has two ways forward:

  • Wait — the default. Do nothing and the game stays frozen, keeping their seat. If they relaunch and re-join, everyone picks up exactly where they left off (a brief “Opponent reconnected” and play resumes).
  • Hand it to the AI — one click gives the silent realm to a Normal AI so the match carries on rather than waiting indefinitely.

If the one who dropped was the host, there's no host left to re-join — so the player still in the game gets a third button, “Take over hosting.” Click it and your machine becomes the host; the game shows a short rejoin code. Read it to the other player and they relaunch with --code=… --seat=1 to slot back into their own realm, and the match resumes with the roles quietly swapped.

Reachability, plainly: the new host has to be reachable at the address it shows — true on a home or LAN network, where you and your friends usually play. Across the open internet it may need the same port-forwarding the original host did.

IV

People & Economy

Your peasants are the engine of everything. Every worker in the fields, every soldier in the line, starts as an idle peasant — so keep a few spare, always.

Gathering

Raise a Woodcutter's Hut by the trees, a Quarry by the rock, an Iron Mine by the rusty ore. Each hires its own peasant, who walks out, gathers a load, and hauls it home — over and over. A Storehouse placed near the work is a closer drop-off, so loads bank faster.

Food & farms

A Farm sows a field and reaps Food straight from the land — one farmer, one field, no chain to staff. Any open ground grows a field worth 1, but the richer soil scattered across your land yields 2 or 3. Press V to see the values and aim your farms at the 3s. A Granary is a food drop-off by the fields, like the storehouse is for timber.

Room to grow

Each House raises how many people your realm can hold. Population isn't bought — it arrives when approval is high and there's room (see The Realm).

The ⚠ marker

Any building that wants a worker but can't find a spare idle peasant flies a small ⚠ until one is free. If your economy stalls, look for the warnings — you've usually run out of hands. Build a House, ease off recruiting, or wait for newcomers.

V

The Realm

Click your keep to open the realm panel. Two dials — Tax and Rations — both move one number: Approval. Keep it above 50% and newcomers arrive; let it sink and idlers drift off (workers always stay).

The trick is that the two dials pull on approval on the same ±9 scale, so they cancel step for step. Crank tax to fill the treasury, then set a richer table to win the approval back — you're trading gold against food.

ApprovalTax settingRations setting
+9Bribe (pays the people)Feast
+6GiftRich
+3NoneHearty

Higher tax fills the treasury (the cruelest settings rake in the most; the lowest actually pay a bribe out); richer rations eat more food each turn. Set a table your larder can't cover and the realm goes hungry — the harshest approval hit there is, whatever the dial says. Let a food buffer build before you raise the table.

Keys: - / = lower and raise tax; [ / ] lower and raise rations.

VII

Your Army

Every soldier is an armed peasant. Click a barracks, pick a unit, and it costs 15 wood and a spare idle peasant, then trains in a few seconds. So army size is really gated by your population — keep people spare, or you can't recruit.

The Scout — your eyes

The Scout is a recon unit, not a fighter. It has the fastest legs on the field, a far-seeing eye (nearly twice a soldier's fog radius), and stealth — enemies can't make it out until they're nearly on top of it, so it slips through the fog unseen. It's frail and feeble in a fight, so send it to find the enemy, never to trade blows. Its wide sight also feeds the cautious march: what a scout reveals, all your armies will route around.

Free roam & reports. Select a scout and press N to set it roaming: it strikes out on its own, always heading for the nearest dark ground, peeling back the fog without a single click from you. The moment it spots the enemy it calls back — a message on screen (“Scout spotted the enemy stronghold — to the north-east”) and a beacon dropped where it saw them, so you can send the army to answer. It reports the biggest thing it sees — an enemy keep over a lesser building over loose troops — and won't spam you: each callout is spaced a good while apart. Give the scout any manual order and it drops out of roam and obeys.

Veterans

Units learn to fight. Each enemy a unit slays is a kill, and kills earn rank: at two kills it becomes a Veteran (★), at five an Elite (★★) — a gold star (or two) floats over it, and its rank shows when you select it. Each rank makes it tougher and its blows harder (+25% at Veteran, +50% at Elite), and a promotion heals it on the spot. So a soldier kept alive through battle after battle becomes worth several fresh recruits — protect your veterans, and pull a wounded elite back to fight another day rather than throwing it away.

Walls & garrisons

Right-click a soldier onto your own wall, turret or keep (with steps to climb) and it mans the rampart — firing farther for the height and taking less damage for the cover. Ramparts buy time against a rush, but they're not permanent; a determined assault breaches a wall in under a minute.

Standing guard

You can't watch every border while you tend the economy — so set a watch. Select some troops and press H to put them on guard: they hold their post, and the moment an enemy sets foot anywhere in your territory they march out to intercept it. Clear the ground and they fall back to their post — a guard holds the line, it doesn't go chasing a fleeing foe off across the map and out of position. Give a guard any manual order and it stands down.

And whether or not you've posted a guard, the realm raises the alarm when a foe crosses your border: “Enemy in your lands!” with a red beacon where they are, so you can rush the main army over. (A Scout's stealth still gets it past your watch — another reason scouting cuts both ways.)

Siege engines

Walls and keeps are tough; soldiers batter them slowly. For a real siege, research Siege Engineering (on the shared war branch — see The Tech Tree), build a Siege Workshop, and engineer a machine — for wood and iron, crewed by a spare peasant. Siege engines wreck buildings many times faster than troops do, but their blows against people are feeble: a single soldier who reaches one will cut it down. So they must be escorted.

  • Ram — armoured and tanky; smashes a gate or wall from right up against it.
  • Catapult — mid-range and fragile; lobs stone at a wall from a few tiles back.
  • Trebuchet — the siege king: enormous range (it out-reaches wall archers) and the hardest blow, so it can level a keep from beyond the walls — but it's slow and made of glass. Keep it alive and it wins sieges single-handed.

Because they cost iron, an iron mine finally earns its keep — and a besieger has a real reason to fight for the ore patches.

Need an army right now and have the coin? The Market hires mercenaries for gold, no peasant required — see the next chapter.

The Court — Prestige & Champions

A renowned court fields heroes. Prestige (✦) is your court's renown — a currency you earn and spend, shown on the top bar. You earn it three ways:

  • Glory in battle — every enemy your troops fell adds Prestige, and a veteran's or champion's kills are worth more. Win fights and your renown grows on its own.
  • The Royal Kitchen — build one and its feasts turn food into a steady trickle of Prestige (and a little popularity — a generous lord is a loved one), while it throws a grand tournament now and then that spends gold for a burst of renown and cheer.
  • Statues — raise a Statue (it costs stone and Prestige) and it stands there radiating a slow, permanent stream of renown. Renown earned in battle, poured into a monument, that pays you back forever — the more you build, the faster your Prestige climbs.

And you spend it on Champions: with a Royal Kitchen standing and the Prestige to pay, press M to muster a Champion — a knight of renown far above a common soldier (tough, hard-hitting), raised at the court gate. Never trained, never bought with gold: the reward for a court that wins its wars and feasts on the glory. Turn your victories into the heroes that win the next one.

Trading & standing orders

You always buy high and sell low — that ±25% gap is the merchant's cut, so churning a good back and forth just bleeds gold. The real power is a standing order: for each good, set it to buy up to N or sell above N, and every turn the market closes the gap on its own. Set it well and your economy runs itself.

Weapons & the self-arming army

Weapons are market-only — no farm or mine makes them. A barracks will arm a recruit from a stocked weapon instead of spending wood, so bought arms are a second road to an army. Combine the two ideas — auto-buy weapons with your tax gold — and your barracks quietly turns coin into soldiers, a war economy that runs itself.

Mercenaries

When you need troops now, hire mercenaries: a trained soldier for gold, with no peasant, no barracks, no wait. They muster at the market. This is the one way past the population gate that limits a normal army — pure gold becomes pure army.

Fair play — why gold can't buy the game

  • Wages, not just a price. Every mercenary draws pay each turn and deserts if you can't pay — so your army is capped by sustainable income, not your hoard. The panel shows your running wage bill.
  • It fights your own crown. Those wages drain the very treasury the Economic path races to 70,000 — a standing army or the gold win, not both.
  • Anyone can hire. The market is open to every path, and tax scales with population (which the 200-pop win-floor guarantees), so a big-population realm can out-earn a merchant.
  • Rivals can bleed it. Every merc cut down is gold you must re-spend — an expensive company is a target, not a wall.
  • An army is a tool, not a path. Troops defend, raid and annex, but you still win by a path — never by force alone.
IX

The Tech Tree

The tree is how you win. Your realm banks research every turn; press C and spend it, node by node, to climb a branch. Each branch ends in a ★ capstone that unlocks its crown — and here's the catch that shapes the whole game: you can capstone only one branch, and a second branch costs ever more the deeper you dig. So one branch to its capstone plus a shallow dip into another is the dual goal you need to win (next chapter).

From the trunk — Roads, which speeds all your research — five gates open, one per ambition:

GateClimb its branchCrown at the top
Markettrade income → Banking → interest on the hoard★ Grand Exchange
Chapelchurches that convert more of your people★ Grand Temple
Scholar's Hutfaster research → the Academy → wonders★ Academy
Farmsteadbigger housing, faster growth, new keeps★ Sovereign's Court
Musterthe shared war branch — spies, war-tools & siege engineering(no crown — a tool for all)

Each branch has a fork — a pick-one sidegrade where taking one path closes the other. And deep in every branch sits a ⚔ war-tool node that makes fighting feed that branch's crown (see Spies & War). The Muster branch is shared: it has no crown and no cross-branch penalty, so any player can reach the spies, war-tools and Siege Engineering (which unlocks the Siege Workshop) no matter which crown they chase.

X

The Four Paths to Victory

This is the heart of the game. Winning is a scored race, not the last keep standing — and it always takes two ambitions, not one.

The dual goal

To win you must reach the HIGH goal of one path and the MEDIUM goal of a different path, and hold the HIGH for a sustained spell. No single-stat runaway takes the crown — every winner has touched two of the four ambitions. Since the tech tree only lets you capstone one branch cheaply, this falls out naturally: go deep on one, dip into a second.

First, be a kingdom

No crown counts until your realm holds at least 200 people — a floor under every path, so no one snaps a quick win from a hamlet. Meet a goal while too small and the tracker shows ⚠ needs 200 pop; the winning hold only begins once you've grown. Build houses and keep approval high to settle them.

CrownHIGH goalMEDIUM goalUnlocked by
Economichold 70,000 goldbank 35,000 once★ Grand Exchange

The crowns in brief

Economic — the Merchant. Climb the Market branch for a trade income on top of tax; Banking pays interest on your hoard, and the Grand Exchange floors it. Amass and hold a great treasury. The longest, steadiest race.

Religious — the Faithful. A Church ministers to your people and raises faith (it opens at 25%). A small, dense town converts fastest — beware that a huge realm needs many churches to convert three-quarters of it. A quick crown to reach, a hard one to hold.

Science — the Scholar. Research speeds itself, then the Academy unlocks Wonders — grand monuments that rise over ~30 seconds, visible and sabotageable the whole time, and count only once finished. Raise two.

Domain — the Sovereign. Swell your population with Homesteads, and use Provincial Keeps to found new keeps — each a fresh territory. The HIGH wants a great census across five lands, taken by settling or by conquest.

Tempo & the alarm

A crown must be held after you reach it — a long vigil, about 1½ hours for the Economic hoard and roughly an hour for the rest. Reaching the goal is only the start; surviving the hold, while rivals research their spies and march their armies, is the real game. All told a match runs on the order of two hours. (That length is a single dial the host sets — an Epic pace by default; a group can crank it shorter or longer.)

The moment anyone crosses 80% of a HIGH goal, the whole realm is told. That announcement is the window for rivals to strike back — with a spy, a raid, or a wonder-wrecking saboteur — before the hold runs out.

XI

Spies & War

When a rival is announced at 80%, a spy is the one thing that pushes their score back. Research a dagger in the Spy Guild (on the shared Muster branch — any path can reach it), press X, and loose it at your rival. Each costs gold and rests on a cooldown.

DaggerStrikesBlunted by
Embezzlerskims their gold into yoursBanking House
Inquisitorknocks their faith backCathedral
Saboteurwrecks a wonderPrinting Press
Agitatordrives their people offProvincial Keeps
Assassincuts down a soldierBodyguard

Notice the counters: each branch's own Tier-III node hardens it against the very dagger meant for it — so climbing toward your crown makes you tougher to knock off it, and the Bodyguard stops the Assassin cold.

War feeds the attacker

Research your branch's ⚔ war-tool and every fight fuels your crown — there's no spite-only griefing here, only war that advances you:

  • Privateers (Economic) — your kills pillage gold into your hoard.
  • War Loot (Science) — strip wood & stone from the fallen to fund wonders.
  • Crusade (Religious) — victory in battle emboldens the faith.
  • Conquest (Domain) — an enemy keep you strike down is annexed, not razed: it becomes a new territory under your banner, and the folk around it change hands. This is Domain's "five lands, taken by force."

You cannot kill the king

Fight a rival all the way and something surprising happens: nothing final. Raze a realm's last keep and its ruler is not eliminated — the king flees into exile. Their fallen territory is razed and their realm reset to a bare opening, and after a short regroup a new keep and a starter camp rise at the safest, most distant corner of the map, where they rebuild.

Why war still matters

It's a brutal setback, not a death. The exiled lord loses their buildings, their treasury, their army's home, and every ounce of victory progress they were holding — while you keep all the loot from the fight. Knocking a leader back to a bare corner while you're a crown deep is devastating. But they keep their researched knowledge and any banked medium goal, so a beaten rival can claw back into the race — no one is ever kingmade out of the game by an early rush.

The Avenger — think twice about the killing blow

Landing that final blow is dangerous. The instant a keep is razed, the exiled king's champion — the Avenger — rises from the ruins, right in the midst of the army that did it. It is a single, immense unit: enormous health, a battering blow, fast legs, out for blood. One Avenger can carve through a tired siege force and wreck the buildings behind it before it falls.

It's the one thing you get for losing everything — and it can never be trained or hired; only exile raises it. So an attacker eyeing the killing blow has to ask whether their army can survive what erupts from the rubble. Sometimes the wiser play is to leave a rival at one keep, bloodied but un-exiled, rather than hand them a monster and a grudge.

XII

Facing the Enemy

Computer lords chase crowns too — and not all the same one. A match can field several bots spread across the four paths, each climbing its branch, raising the structures its crown wants, and wielding its war-tool.

The tougher ones play the whole board. A Hard lord will:

  • run the spy ring — locking a dagger onto you the moment you're announced at 80%, and keeping that score suppressed through your hold;
  • rush a Bodyguard if it catches you training an Assassin;
  • use the market to convert its income into arms and mercenaries.

Treat an announced rival as a threat, and expect to be treated as one the instant you cross 80%.

XIII

A Lord's Counsel

A few hard-won lessons to carry into your first games.

The opening

Bootstrap the economy first: a woodcutter or two, a farm on good soil (press V and hunt the 3s), and a couple of houses so people keep arriving. Keep approval comfortably above 50% — a growing town is the foundation of every crown, and you need 200 souls before any of them count.

Pick your race early

Because you can only capstone one branch cheaply, decide your HIGH goal early and commit the research to it — then choose a second path for your MEDIUM. Religious and Science crown fast but must survive a long hold; Economic and Domain build slower but are harder to knock off the mark. Play to your temperament.

Scout, then march

Keep a Scout out. Its sight reveals threats — which both warns you and lets your other armies take a cautious march around them. Marching blind into fog is how good armies die.

Defend the announcement

Crossing 80% rings a bell for every rival. Have your defence ready before you tip over — the counters live in your own branch's Tier-III node, so climbing your crown is also how you armour it. And keep a little gold back: mercenaries can plug a hole in a hurry.

Don't over-hire

Mercenaries are a hammer, not a home. They draw wages and desert if the treasury runs dry — and every coin on the payroll is a coin not in your victory hoard. Buy them for a job, not to sit.
